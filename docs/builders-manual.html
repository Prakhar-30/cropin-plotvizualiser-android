Finding a boundary that isn't there

Two pieces of software. One draws a farm plot on satellite imagery; the other stands in that field and shows you where its edges run and where its crop needs attention, through the camera, with no signal — using ARCore only to track the phone's own motion, never its Geospatial service. This is how both work, and why each part is built the way it is.

Sheet 01

The problem

A field agent is dispatched to plot PLT-7013. The boundary exists — as eight pairs of coordinates in a database. On the ground it is marked by nothing at all.

Farm plots in most of India are not fenced. They are separated by a footpath, a change of crop, or an agreement between two families. An agent arriving to inspect one faces three questions in order, and the tooling answers none of them well:

  • Which field is mine? A pin on a maps app puts a dot at the centroid. Standing in a row of six visually identical rectangles, a dot fifty metres away does not tell you which one it belongs to.
  • Where does it end? This is the question that costs money. Fertiliser applied ten metres past a boundary is applied to somebody else's crop, and a yield estimate that includes a neighbour's headland is wrong before anyone has done any agronomy.
  • Which part of it is failing? Satellite imagery can already answer this from orbit. It arrives as a raster on a laptop in an office, and the agent standing on the actual soil has no way to know they are looking at the bad patch.

Why the obvious answers were rejected

ARCore Geospatial API. Google's own answer to this, and genuinely excellent — where it works. It resolves your pose by matching the camera against Street View imagery, so it needs Street View coverage, a live network connection at the moment of use, and a Play Services download. Rural farmland has none of the first and unreliable amounts of the second. A tool that degrades to nothing at the site it was built for is not a tool.

That rejection is of the Geospatial service only. Since the October field tests the app does use ARCore's plain motion tracking — on-device visual-inertial odometry that needs no Street View, no network and no API key — because GNSS alone could not hold a boundary still at close range. Sheet 04 explains why, and the app runs without it on phones that lack it.

RTK / external GNSS. Centimetre accuracy, and the correct answer if someone is paying for a survey. It is a second device, a subscription to a correction network, and a training burden, for a job whose tolerance is a couple of metres.

A better map screen. Cheap and already tried. Looking down at a top-down map and then up at a field requires the agent to do the projection in their head, which is exactly the step that goes wrong.

Sheet 02

The solution

Draw the boundary where it actually is — standing up, in the camera, at the right distance — using the phone's own GNSS, motion sensors and on-device camera tracking — nothing from the network once the plot is cached.

The system is deliberately split in two, and the split is the most important architectural decision in the project:

Web tool
Creates truthBoundaries are drawn here and only here. The phone is read-only.
Mobile app
Finds truthCaches boundaries, then locates them on the ground with the radio off.

One writer means there is never a merge conflict between two people's idea of where a fence runs. It also means the phone can be built as a pure consumer: no editing UI, no conflict resolution, no upload queue for geometry. What it does need is to work at the bottom of a field with one bar of signal, which is why everything it draws comes out of a local database.

Why this helps, concretely

  • It works where the crop is. No Street View and no live network. ARCore's on-device tracking where Google Play Services for AR is installed, plain phone sensors where it is not. Sync at the office or at the gate; the visit itself needs nothing.
  • It runs on the phone the agent already carries. Android 7.0 or newer (the plugin's floor, set by ARCore), about 9 MB as a release build. No hardware to procure, lose or charge.
  • It shows a shape, not a point. An outline can be matched against the real hedge line, which means the agent can confirm the app is right — and catch it when it is wrong. A pin offers no such check.
  • It carries crop condition to the soil. Crop greenness (NDVI), crop vigour (NDRE) and water stress (LSWI) are each graded into normal, moderate and critical zones. Every moderate or critical patch in every nearby plot stands up as a drop point taller than the boundary walls, so a problem two fields away is visible over the fence.
  • It is honest about its own error. Every reading carries where the heading came from, how old it is, and the expected error in degrees. Near a boundary the drawn edge widens to the live GNSS accuracy rather than pretending to a crisp line.
  • It asks the agent for nothing. There is no access point and no button. Open the camera, walk; within 10 m of any plot's boundary the view anchors itself to the nearest point on that boundary, and direction is worked out from the walk.
Stated plainly

The crop-health values in the system today are synthetic — deterministic noise seeded from the plot id, shaped so that 85–90% of a plot reads normal with a few believable moderate and critical patches, separately for NDVI, NDRE and LSWI. They are not derived from satellite imagery. Every API response carries synthetic: true and a note saying so, and the app labels it "demo values" on screen. Wiring real Sentinel-2 indices means replacing one function, syntheticSamplers; nothing downstream of it knows where the numbers came from.

Sheet 03

How the web platform works

Three npm workspaces — shared, service, web — where shared is the geometry both of the other two are compiled against, so the browser and the server can never disagree about an area.

Drawing a plot, click by click

The map screen is MapLibre GL rendering a bare raster style: Esri World Imagery and nothing else. No basemap labels, because a farm boundary is matched against hedges and irrigation channels, not against place names.

All drawing interaction lives in a controller class outside React. The map owns the DOM; React only renders the numbers the controller reports. That separation exists because MapLibre and React both want to own the same canvas, and letting React re-render vertices produced dropped clicks.

What happens on save

The browser POSTs the ring. The server does not trust a single number in it.

  1. Validate. One validator, in shared, run by both sides: ring closed, at least three distinct vertices, no self-intersection, area greater than zero and under the 500 ha ceiling, name and note within length limits.
  2. Recompute. Area and centroid are calculated server-side from the ring with Turf and written from that calculation — never taken from the request body. A client that lies about its area cannot persist the lie.
  3. Store once. The boundary goes into PostGIS as geography(Polygon,4326). Bounding boxes, radius searches and distances are derived from it by PostGIS on demand rather than stored alongside, so there is no second copy to drift. area_sq_m is the one persisted derivative, because it is the number a surveyor agreed to — and a CHECK constraint rejects any row whose stored area differs from the true geodesic area by more than 1%.
  4. Assign a colour. fieldar.pick_colour_index() picks a palette slot 0–7 that no other plot within 500 m is using, so adjacent plots are never the same colour on screen. The database stores the slot number, not the colour — re-theming both clients is then a palette edit, not a data migration.
Migration policy

Everything lives in its own fieldar schema. Migrations 001 and 002 are additive only — CREATE … IF NOT EXISTS or CREATE OR REPLACE. Migration 003_updated_flow.sql is the one deliberate exception: it starts the data fresh for the new flow, deleting every row in fieldar.plots, dropping the access_point column and narrowing severity to 0–2, in one transaction. It touches nothing outside the fieldar schema and its public.fieldar_* wrappers.

The crop-condition raster

GET /api/plots/:id/health builds the grid on demand — 3 m cells, three indices per cell — and caches it by (plot, capture date). Because the samplers are deterministic, that cache key is complete — the same plot always produces the same raster, so the cache never has to be invalidated. The grid, the zone classification and the patch clustering all live in shared, which is what a real Supabase ingest job would call too. That is the reason they are there rather than in the route: the map and the database must never disagree about which cell is stressed.

On the map, the Crop condition panel — on by default — grades every saved plot at once and switches between crop greenness, crop vigour and water stress. For all plots together, the moderate and critical cells are painted yellow and red and every patch is a drop point on the map (click one for its plot, zone, area and coordinate); normal cells are left unpainted, as in the camera. The panel gives the share of all graded ground in each zone and lists the drop points, worst first. Snapshots are fetched four at a time and kept, so switching index costs nothing. Under 720 px the side panels become a bottom sheet with tabs (Plots, Draw, Condition) and the drawing tools gain Undo corner and Close shape, because a phone has no right-click or double-click to spare.

Sheet 04

How the mobile app works

A Flutter plugin that a host app opens with one widget. Every read goes to the local database and nothing on screen waits for the network. In the field the agent is asked to do nothing but walk.

The mobile side is now a Flutter package, plot_finder, with its standalone app in example/. All of the reasoning — frames, filters, heading, geometry — is plain Dart under lib/core and runs in unit tests with no device. The Android plumbing is the package's own Kotlin plugin: location from the framework's LocationManager, rotation vectors, the accelerometer, permissions, camera geometry, and the ARCore view that draws the camera image and the plot. A host app such as Cropin Grow adds one dependency and pushes PlotFinderScreen; see INTEGRATION.md.

The agent's moments

  1. Sync, then start. The app opens on a home screen: how many plots the phone holds, how fresh they are, Sync now, and Start AR view. It syncs by itself on opening when there are no plots or they are over an hour old. No plot is picked — every cached plot within 400 m is drawn, the nearest 24 at most.
  2. Wait a few seconds, visibly. A card over the camera ticks off Camera, Motion tracking and GPS, with a seconds counter, and after 20 s says to step into the open. The location stream sends the phone's last-known fix the moment it opens if it is under 30 s old, so usually there is almost no wait.
  3. Walk. Until the view is anchored, a pill points at the nearest boundary of the nearest plot and gives the distance. While the agent walks, the app silently works out where north is from the shape of the walk (logic 11). No button.
  4. Anchor, automatically. When GPS puts the agent within 10 m of any plot's boundary the app takes the nearest point on that boundary, whether a corner or a point computed along an edge, as where the agent is, and pins the position there (logic 12). The sheet says Anchored to the X boundary. It re-anchors after 60 m of walking or a loss of tracking.
  5. Look. Every nearby plot's boundary stands up as walls; the moderate (yellow) and critical (red) 3 m cells of the chosen index are painted on the ground inside them; each patch of them stands up as a 4.5 m drop point, taller than the 2.6 m walls; people and trees in front hide them; and a minimap in the corner shows all of it from above with the agent as a blue arrow. Normal ground is not drawn — only what needs a visit.
  6. Switch the index. Crop greenness (NDVI), crop vigour (NDRE) or water stress (LSWI), from the bottom sheet. The drop points and minimap change together.

The frame loop

On the ARCore path there are two loops. The GL thread runs at camera rate: it draws the camera image, then draws the plot with that same frame's view and projection matrices, so the boundary cannot lag the picture — the sliding corner seen in the third field test was exactly that lag, when the plot was still painted by Flutter a frame or two late. Dart runs at sensor and fix rate: it updates the position and heading, and sends the renderer a new placement only when the scene's registration on the earth has actually moved (an anchor, a heading lock, a GNSS drift correction). All nearby plots share one local frame, so one placement moves them together. Walking does not move it, because the agent's position and ARCore's camera move together. Flutter repaints only what is text or furniture: the label, the guide pill, the minimap's arrow.

Without ARCore — an unsupported phone, or a session that fails to start — the screen falls back by itself to the original design: the camera plugin's preview, rotation vectors and counted steps, projected in Dart and painted on a Flutter canvas.

One thing worth being precise about, because it is the question the team will ask first: the camera does not recognise anything. ARCore tracks feature points in the image to measure how the phone moves, measures depth for occlusion, and finds the floor plane; nothing looks for fields, fences or crops. Where the plot is comes from its stored coordinates, GNSS and the automatic boundary anchor. That is why a crop row cannot move the boundary — and why the app cannot correct a wrong boundary by looking at the scene.

Sheet 05

The logics, one by one

Eleven pieces of reasoning that between them account for nearly all of the code that is not UI. Each is here because a specific thing went wrong without it. File paths are the Flutter package's; the web's are unchanged.

01 · Local metric frame (ENU)

lib/core/geo/enu.dart · shared/src/constants.ts
What it does

Converts WGS84 degrees into a flat East–North–Up frame in metres, anchored at one GNSS fix. Every distance, bearing, projection and filter in the app runs in that frame. Degrees are converted once, at the edge, and never used for arithmetic again.

Why it exists

A degree of latitude is 110,540 m everywhere. A degree of longitude is 111,320 m at the equator and about 108,600 m at Mandya. Anything computed in raw degrees is therefore wrong by a different amount on each axis — a grid built that way is visibly rectangular instead of square, and a variance expressed in degrees would make a Kalman filter anisotropic for no physical reason.

The frame is immutable. Moving the anchor means constructing a new one, which makes it impossible to convert two points against different origins by accident. Once the agent has walked 50 m from the origin, the frame re-anchors: the estimate and its variance are physical quantities and survive untouched, so re-anchoring is invisible on screen.

This is the answer to "how do I compute the distance"

Subtract the two positions in degrees, scale each axis by its metres-per-degree, then take the hypotenuse. That is distanceM(from, to) in four lines. The equirectangular approximation is accurate to well under a metre at the few-hundred-metre ranges this app works over — far inside the GNSS error it is drawn against. On the web side, where there is no real-time budget, distances and areas are geodesic instead, computed by Turf on the ellipsoid.

02 · Pinhole projection and near-plane clipping

lib/core/geo/projection.dart · camera_geometry.dart · arcore_pose.dart
What it does

Turns a ground coordinate into a screen pixel. Translate the point into the camera's frame, rotate it into device axes with the sensor's own rotation matrix, then divide x and y by depth and scale by the focal length in pixels.

Why it exists in this form

The focal length is not guessed. CameraCharacteristics gives the physical sensor size and the lens focal length in millimetres, from which the true field of view follows; the app logs it on startup so it can be read off a real handset. Assuming a typical FOV instead scales the entire overlay by whatever the error is, which reads as the boundary being at the wrong distance.

On the ARCore path the question goes away: the plot is drawn by ARCore's own projection matrix, and the label's field of view is read back from it (fov = 2·atan(1/P[0])), so the overlay is drawn with exactly the lens geometry of the picture under it.

Two details are load-bearing. The camera position is a required argument with no default — an earlier version took only the rotation and silently placed the camera at the frame origin, which welded the boundary to the anchor so it appeared to travel alongside the agent while the distance label counted honestly down. Points behind the camera must be culled, because a negative depth mirrors them into the frame in front: the field behind you renders on top of the field you are looking at.

Culling alone is not enough for a polygon. If the agent stands inside the plot and you simply drop the two corners behind them, the remaining outline closes across the middle of the field. So the ring is clipped against the near plane instead, introducing new vertices on the plane — Sutherland–Hodgman, and the reason walls are wound as quads.

03 · Position stabiliser

lib/core/geo/position_stabilizer.dart · lib/core/motion/
What it does

A constant-position Kalman filter, one scalar filter per horizontal axis, gated by a zero-velocity detector — the standard construction from pedestrian navigation, not an invention. Two numbers produce the whole behaviour: process noise says how fast the true position may be changing (2 cm/s standing, 1.6 m/s walking), and measurement noise is the receiver's own reported accuracy, squared.

Why it exists

A raw GNSS fix wanders several metres from one second to the next even on a phone lying motionless on a wall. Fed straight into the camera pose, the boundary swims — which a farmer reads as "the app does not know where my field is", and they are not wrong to read it that way.

Standing still, the estimate's variance stays small, the Kalman gain collapses toward zero, and new fixes barely move the estimate. Walking, the variance opens up and the estimate tracks. There is no threshold to tune between the two.

Three guards around it

Accuracy floor. Android receivers routinely claim 1 m when the true error is several. No measurement is ever treated as sharper than 3 m, or the filter becomes over-confident and starts ignoring good fixes.

Outlier gate. An innovation beyond 3σ is rejected and the estimate held — but only for four consecutive fixes or five seconds, whichever comes first. A count alone is not a bound: four samples is four seconds at 1 Hz and forever at 0 Hz, which would strand the estimate on a stale position after one wild fix.

Slew limiter. The newest piece, and the one that closed out field testing. The Kalman estimate is built to be unbiased, which is right for an estimator and wrong for something a person is looking at: each fix nudges it a few centimetres and the overlay twitches with it. So the drawn position was separated from the estimate. The estimate tracks the receiver; the drawn position chases it at 8 cm/s while stationary, 2 m/s while walking, and snaps outright past 10 m so a genuine relocation is not hidden. The cap is on distance along the line to the estimate, not per axis — a per-axis cap lets a diagonal correction travel √2 times the intended rate and bends the path toward the axes, which is visible as the overlay sidling rather than sliding.

What the field tests added

Counted steps. The first indoor test walked 9 m towards a plot and the boundary walked away: under a roof the receiver reported near-zero Doppler speed, the filter took the agent for standing still, and froze. Steps are now detected from the accelerometer (peak-valley on its magnitude), sized by Weinberg's model with a gain calibrated against good GNSS, and each one moves the estimate and the drawn position at once. Steps, not Doppler, decide moving versus still.

Camera tracking. Where ARCore runs, its frame-to-frame camera displacement replaces the steps entirely: measured, not modelled, to centimetres. While it flows, fixes count three times less and the drawn position absorbs GNSS corrections at no more than 5 cm/s, so a receiver drifting under a roof cannot slide the boundary sideways as the agent closes in.

The anchor. The boundary anchor (logic 12) replaces the estimate with the nearest point on the boundary outright, and fixes then count ten times less again. It used to be a button at a surveyed access point; it is now automatic and fires only within 10 m of a boundary by GPS, so it cannot place the agent in a field they are not beside.

04 · Orientation smoother

lib/core/geo/orientation_smoother.dart · fallback path
What it does

Spherical interpolation between quaternions with a time constant that adapts to how far the estimate has fallen behind — the 1-euro filter (Casiez, Roussel & Vogel, CHI 2012) applied to attitude. Heavy when the phone is nearly still (τ = 0.65 s), light when it is being turned (τ = 0.05 s).

Why it matters more than the position filter

Position error moves the overlay by a fixed number of metres. Orientation error moves it by an amount that grows with range: at 50 m, one degree of jitter swings a boundary 0.87 m sideways; at 150 m it is 2.6 m. Android's rotation vector wobbles by a degree or two even on a table, so an unfiltered pose is worth several metres of visible movement — considerably more than the raw GNSS jitter that was fixed first.

Interpolation is on quaternions, not matrices. Averaging rotation matrices element-wise does not produce a rotation: the result is not orthonormal and it shears the overlay rather than turning it. The matrix-to-quaternion conversion uses Shepperd's method, branching on the largest diagonal term — not an optimisation, but because the naive form divides by a value approaching zero for rotations near 180°, which is exactly a phone turned to face behind the agent.

The one subtle decision

The filter adapts on deviation from the current estimate, not on frame-to-frame angular rate. Jitter of a degree and a half arriving every 20 ms is an instantaneous rate of 75 °/s, which looks exactly like a deliberate pan and defeats a rate-adaptive filter completely. (On the ARCore path this filter is not needed: ARCore's attitude is visually tracked and already steady. It still runs on the sensor fallback.) Deviation separates them cleanly: jitter is bounded by its own amplitude however fast it arrives, because the estimate sits in the middle of it, while a genuine turn makes the estimate fall behind and the gap grows until the filter speeds up to close it.

Field testing raised the deviation threshold from 10° to 25°. Walking is not a still phone with noise on top — each footfall pitches and rolls the handset several degrees and an arm swing adds more, so ordinary gait was reading as deliberate motion and the boundary jogged along with the walk. A deliberate pan is 60° or more and still clears the higher bar immediately.

05 · Heading fusion

lib/core/heading/ · heading_fusion.dart · trajectory_aligner.dart · course_over_ground_detector.dart
What it does

Establishes true north from something trustworthy, then carries it on the gyroscope. Three sources, ranked, each reported to the user by name with its own expected error.

Why the compass is not enough

A magnetometer near a tractor, a transformer, a steel gatepost or a phone case with a magnet is wrong by 15–25°. As FIG 08 shows, 20° at 80 m puts the boundary 29 m sideways — the middle of the neighbour's field, drawn confidently. So heading is never reported as a bare number; it always carries where it came from and how much to trust it.

The three sources

Course over ground (3–5°). Location.bearing is derived from GNSS motion, not from the magnetic field, so nothing on the ground can corrupt it. The detector accumulates a window and requires 20 m of walking, at least five samples, speed above 0.7 m/s, accuracy better than 25 m, no gap longer than 5 s — and a mean absolute bearing spread under 8°. That last test is what makes it safe: a window where the agent turned would average its two legs into a confident heading pointing between them, so the window is discarded outright rather than averaged.

The walk itself (a few degrees). On the ARCore path, the trajectory aligner (logic 11) — the tracked walk laid over the GNSS fixes taken along it. The landmark snap that used to sit here (aim at the access point and tap) was removed with the access point.

Magnetometer (15–25°). Fallback, and never a resting state. Once a fix exists the compass is demoted to trimming slow gyro drift — 300 s time constant, and a hard ±10° ceiling on how far it may ever pull a lock. Without that ceiling a magnetically disturbed site would walk a good lock onto the neighbouring field slowly enough that nobody notices.

Two sensors, one job each

TYPE_GAME_ROTATION_VECTOR has no magnetometer input: smooth, disturbance-proof, and arbitrary in absolute yaw. That is the carrier. TYPE_ROTATION_VECTOR knows where north is and feeds only the trim. Only the carrier is smoothed and only the carrier publishes a pose — running both through one filter was a real bug that quietly disabled the smoother, because the two attitudes sit tens of degrees apart in yaw and the deviation measure saturated permanently.

On the ARCore path

ARCore's yaw becomes the carrier instead of the game rotation vector. It is visually tracked and drifts well under a degree a minute, so two rules change: a lock lasts the whole visit rather than lapsing to the compass after three minutes, and the compass gets no authority over a lock — indoors and beside steel it rotated the plot about a standing agent. Before any lock the compass offset is smoothed over 3 s so a compass-placed plot does not shake.

The walk lock is replaced by the trajectory aligner (logic 11), which needs no straight walk. There is no aiming fallback any more: where GPS is too weak for the aligner, direction stays on the compass and the chip says so.

06 · What gets drawn, and why it is a wall

lib/core/render/overlay_geometry.dart · world_scene.dart · android/…/ArOverlayRenderer.kt
What it does

Every nearby plot has its boundary drawn as a line on the ground, on a dark backing, and a post at each corner. The plot the agent is in or nearest to — the primary plot — also has each edge stood up into a vertical panel 2.6 m tall, eye height plus a metre, with a solid rail along its top. The other plots keep only the ground line and 1.2 m posts. Beyond 30 m from the nearest edge the outline is the plot's shape; inside 30 m each edge also gets a band as wide as the live GNSS accuracy.

Keeping neighbours apart

The second field test put two adjoining plots side by side, one drawn red and one yellow, and three things went wrong at once. Their colours read exactly like a critical patch beside a moderate one. Their tinted floors covered the whole screen when the agent stood inside one. And their walls on the shared edge stood in the same place, so the two translucent colours mixed into a third.

So: plot colours are blues, violets and white only, and red and yellow always mean the crop (logic 09). Plots have no floor tint; the only colour on the ground is crop condition. Walls and posts stand 0.5 m inside each plot's own boundary, so a shared edge shows as two fences a metre apart, each in its own plot's colour on its own side — the boundary line itself stays exactly on the boundary. Full walls go only on the primary plot, so a row of neighbouring fences cannot stack into a wall of colour. And each plot's label stands on a point inside it, with a stalk to a dot on the ground and a strip of the plot's colour; labels are placed nearest first, and a later one is lifted clear of any it would overlap.

Why not a line on the ground

The first build drew the boundary flat, and reviewers' first comment was that it read as a coloured carpet rather than a limit — a shape lying on the floor gives the eye no cue that it is an edge. A flat overlay also disappears entirely when the phone is held level, because the ground plane is then edge-on to the camera.

Wall height matters too. A wall topping out below eye level reads as floating: the eye takes the top rail for the boundary and the bottom edge for a shadow, because nothing connects it to the ground. Carrying it past eye level puts the rail against the sky or the far treeline, and the panel unmistakably stands on the earth.

The near mode, and honesty about error

A crisp line at 5 m is a false precision claim, and the first time a farmer disputes a reading it is the line that gets blamed. So inside 30 m the edge becomes a band whose half-width is the receiver's live accuracy, built in metres so perspective narrows it with distance on its own. Bands are suppressed once the agent is closer to the edge than the accuracy itself — a band 8 m wide seen from 6 m has its near side behind the camera and floods the whole frame.

Details that only field testing produces

Painter's ordering. Translucent panels composited in ring order let the far side of a plot paint over the near side, which looks like a crease. Walls are sorted far-to-near.

Fades with floors. A 2.6 m wall 3 m away subtends most of the frame, and a boundary you are standing against is the one you least need drawn over your view. Panels fade out below 8 m — but the rail never drops below 0.75 opacity, because the line is the boundary and losing it as you step up to the fence removes the answer at the moment you are trying to stand on it.

Dark backing strokes. Every rail, post and marker is drawn twice: a dark halo, then the colour over it. A single coloured line disappears wherever the background shares its luminance, and outdoor footage is mostly bright sky, pale soil and corrugated fencing. Road signs and map labels have been drawn this way for a century.

Drop points

Each moderate or critical patch is one drop point: a stem from the ground to 4.5 m and a round head on top, yellow or red, with a dark halo. 4.5 m is deliberate — taller than the 2.6 m walls, so a patch inside a plot beyond the near one shows over its fence, which is the whole point of seeing every nearby plot at once. The head is a screen-space point whose size scales with distance only between limits (35–130% of 44 px), so a far marker stays findable and a near one does not fill the frame. Normal ground gets no marker: the view answers "where must I go", not "what is fine". Drop points come from patches within 300 m, 60 at most, nearest first.

The cells themselves lie on the ground under the drop points — yellow at 60% and red at 80% opacity, under the boundary lines — for every nearby plot at once, but only once the view is anchored (logic 12). Before that the scene is placed from raw GPS and moves with it, and a 3 m square that wanders by several metres paints the wrong ground; a drop point is a target, not a claim about which square metre is red, so drop points show from the start. When ARCore loses tracking the anchor is dropped and the cells go with it until the next one. Only moderate and critical cells are sent: a green carpet across several plots hid the walls, and is not what anyone walks out to see. On the ARCore path they are one triangle list in the shared scene frame, ghosted like any surface when something stands in front; on the sensor path they are projected per frame to 120 m.

Drawn in ARCore's frame, with occlusion

No lag. On the ARCore path the walls, rails, posts, boundary lines, graded cells and drop points are drawn by a small native GL renderer, after the camera image and with that frame's own camera matrices. Dart sends the scene — every nearby plot, triangulated by ear clipping, plus the drop points, all in one shared metric frame — when the set of plots or the index changes, and a placement (a 2-D rotation and offset into ARCore's world) only when the registration moves. The walls of all plots are sorted far-to-near together, so a near plot is never painted over by a far one.

Real ground. The ground is the lowest horizontal plane ARCore finds at least 0.8 m below the phone, not an assumed 1.6 m below wherever the phone is — so raising or lowering the phone no longer moves the plot.

Occlusion. Where the Depth API is available, each overlay fragment is compared with the measured depth at its pixel. Only something clearly in front hides it — nearer than 80% of the overlay's distance starts to, 60% fully does — so a person at 2 m hides a boundary at 16 m while the floor under the fill and the crop just inside the boundary do not. Hidden parts are not removed but ghosted ("X-ray" rendering): lines keep 60% opacity, surfaces 35%, because a boundary that vanished behind the crop it encloses would be no use. Depth reaches about 8 m; beyond it the plot is drawn normally. The badge says "occlusion on" when it is active.

Colour kept. Under ARCore the plot keeps its identity colour at 85% strength or more whatever the heading confidence: a grey translucent wall was simply invisible in a white office and under a pale sky.

07 · The condition grid — 3 m cells, three indices

shared/src/health.ts · service/src/routes/health.ts
The short answer

3 × 3 m cells, fixed, for every plot. Each cell carries three values — NDVI, NDRE and LSWI — and each value is classified into a zone: normal, moderate or critical. The earlier build sized cells per plot (√area ÷ 15, 2–10 m); this flow fixes them at 3 m so that every plot, and every drop point, is on the same ground scale.

The three indices and their zones

Each index is shown to the agent by what it means, not by its acronym. Two edges per index split it into three zones; below the lower edge is critical, between the edges moderate, above the upper edge normal. The edges are conservative on purpose: calling healthy ground a problem sends an agent on a pointless walk, which is how a tool like this loses its audience.

How a cell is built

The grid is laid out in metres, anchored at the polygon's south-west corner, so cells are square on the ground rather than square in degrees. A cell is kept only if its centre falls inside the boundary — which is what keeps every patch centroid a point inside the plot. Then the kept square is intersected with the boundary, because keeping a cell on its centre still leaves up to half of it hanging over the fence, and the layer's whole claim is that it describes this plot.

A 0.17 ha demo plot is about 190 cells. The synthetic samplers place a few stress blobs per index, independently, tuned so that 85–90% of a plot reads normal — believable, not alarming.

08 · Patch clustering — one drop point per patch

shared/src/health.ts · findPatches()
What it does

Per index, flood-fills touching cells in the moderate or critical zone into patches, discards patches smaller than two cells (one 3 m cell alone is as likely to be noise as a problem), then ranks them: worst zone first, then largest, then lowest mean value. A patch's zone is its worst cell, not its average — a patch with a dead centre matters more than its edges suggest — and its drop point stands on the centroid of those worst cells, so a red marker never lands on yellow ground.

Why four-way connectivity, not eight

Diagonal-only neighbours share a single corner on the ground. Chaining through them merges two genuinely separate problems into one patch and puts its centroid in the healthy gap between them — a walk-to point that is not a problem at all. Four-way keeps them separate and gives the agent two drop points to visit.

Why ties break deterministically

Two visits to the same plot must produce the same drop points in the same order, or the agent cannot trust that the marker they walked to last week is the one they see today. The web panel lists patches in this rank.

09 · One palette, two languages

palette.json → tools/gen-palette.mjs → palette.ts · mirrored in lib/core/render/plot_palette.dart
What it does

palette.json is the single source of truth for every colour and opacity in both codebases. A generator reads it and writes the TypeScript, and CI can run it with --check to fail a build that drifted. The Flutter app's plot_palette.dart mirrors the same values by hand and says so at the top of the file; keep the two in step when a colour changes.

Why the colours are what they are

Both backgrounds this app runs against — satellite imagery of farmland, and a phone camera pointed at a field — are dominated by green and brown. There is no green in the plot palette: a green boundary on a green crop is the one combination guaranteed to disappear. Nor is there red, orange, yellow or pink: those are crop condition, and a red plot next to a yellow one read in the field exactly like a critical patch next to a moderate one. The eight slots are blue, cyan, purple, white, magenta, indigo, lavender and orchid. White is included because the first field test happened after dark, where it reads better than any hue. A test fails the build if any saturated slot falls outside the cyan-to-violet hues.

The severity ramp originally ran green → yellow → amber → brown, chosen so the bad end described the ground rather than alarming about it. Outdoors that reasoning failed on its own terms: amber and brown are the colour of dry soil, so the two worst bands were painting earth-colour onto earth and vanishing. A grading layer has to contrast with the ground it grades, not match it. The bad end became red, and with three zones the ramp is now simply green, yellow, red — the colours everyone already reads as fine, watch, act.

The guard, and why it is there

This mechanism failed silently once and it is worth recording. When the severity ramp was first added to the generator, the values were interpolated while editing the generator and pasted in as literals. The output looked perfect, --check passed forever after — because the generator and the files it wrote were equally stale — and a colour change committed to palette.json reached neither language. A build was shipped and field-tested with the old ramp.

The generator now asserts, before writing anything, that every colour in palette.json actually appears in each generated file, and exits with a named diff if not. The lesson generalises: a single-source-of-truth tool whose verification step is part of the same stale artefact verifies nothing.

10 · Offline-first cache and what sync actually costs

lib/data/plot_repository.dart · lib/data/db/app_database.dart
What it does

The sqflite database is the source of truth. Every screen reads from it and nothing on screen ever waits for the network. Sync is an explicit action: fetch plots within 5 km of the agent, replace the cached set, then fetch each plot's health raster concurrently, tolerating individual failures.

Design decisions worth defending

Rasters are a separate endpoint. A health snapshot is an order of magnitude larger than a boundary and has a different lifetime — a boundary is drawn once, a raster is re-derived whenever new imagery lands. Bundling them would force a re-download of every boundary to refresh one raster. Moving from one index to three per cell roughly doubles a raster's size; the figures below are from the single-index build and are kept as an order of magnitude.

Raster failures do not fail the sync. Crop condition is an extra. The AR screen also fetches, quietly, any snapshot a nearby plot is missing when it opens. A sync that lands every boundary and misses one raster is a success; reporting it as a failure sends the agent hunting for signal they do not need. The counts are reported separately so a shortfall is visible rather than looking like plots that happen to be healthy.

An empty radius widens to everything. "0 plots near you" is indistinguishable on screen from "the sync failed", and the honest reading of an empty radius is usually that the boundary was drawn somewhere else.

Timeouts are short — 5 s connect, 8 s read. At a rural site the radio either works within a couple of seconds or it does not work at all, and a request hanging for thirty seconds is a spinner the agent watches instead of a cached plot they could already be walking to.

11 · Direction from the walk

lib/core/heading/trajectory_aligner.dart
What it does

Finds true north without asking the agent to aim at anything. ARCore tracks the shape of the walk exactly, but in a frame whose north is arbitrary; GNSS knows where north is but places each point metres out. The rotation that best lays the tracked path over the GNSS fixes taken along it is the missing yaw — a weighted two-dimensional Procrustes fit, solved in closed form: centre both point sets on their weighted means, and the angle of Σ w·(truth)·conj(tracked), treating each east–north pair as a complex number, is the rotation.

Why it replaced the walk lock

The sensor path's course-over-ground lock needs 20 m of straight walking, because GNSS bearing is only meaningful along a straight leg. A real approach winds between bunds. With the tracked path supplying the shape, any route counts in full, and accuracy grows with the lever arm: in the tests a winding 60 m approach with ±5 m fixes recovers the direction to within a few degrees.

Field agents were also clear that aiming at a point and tapping, as a separate step, made no sense to them. The aligner makes direction a by-product of walking.

The boundary anchor, and the guards

When the app anchors to a boundary point (logic 12), that point enters the fit as an anchor. It pins the fit's centre, so every step away from it — in any direction — refines the yaw; about 35 m of walking is enough. The fit weights it as known to a metre, as it did the surveyed access point — but the point was chosen from a GPS fix, so its error is the fix's error, not a metre (see logic 12).

The fit reports its own error: the rotation's variance is the inverse of Σ w·r² (each fix's weight times its squared distance from the centre), inflated five-fold because successive fixes are correlated rather than independent, and widened further when the fixes scatter more than their claimed accuracy. A fit is offered as a lock only under 10°. Fixes worse than 15 m are ignored — indoors, at ±50–100 m, the aligner says nothing rather than lock a guess — and a loss of ARCore tracking discards everything measured before it, since the session's frame may have shifted.

12 · The automatic boundary anchor

lib/core/geo/boundary_snap.dart · lib/ui/ar/ar_controller.dart
What it does

Replaces the access point. For every nearby plot it finds the nearest point on the boundary to the agent: for each edge, the foot of the perpendicular from the agent, clamped to the edge's ends — so it is a corner when a corner is nearest and a computed point along an edge otherwise. The nearest of those across all plots is the candidate. When GPS puts the agent within 10 m of it, the position is pinned there and the trajectory aligner takes it as its anchor. No button, no confirmation. Deep inside a large plot, more than 10 m from every edge, nothing fires until the agent nears an edge; the plots are still drawn from GPS meanwhile.

When it fires again

Once per approach. It re-anchors after 60 m of walking from the last anchor — a different plot, or the far side of the same one — and after ARCore loses tracking, because the session's frame may have moved. Between anchors, ARCore carries the position to centimetres.

What it can and cannot fix — stated plainly

The anchor does not add information GPS did not have. A surveyed access point was a place the agent confirmed standing on; the nearest boundary point is chosen from the GPS fix, so if the fix is 6 m out, the anchor is chosen from a position 6 m out. What it does buy is stillness and a shared frame: from the anchor on, the overlay stops following GPS wander and ARCore holds it, and every plot is registered from the same point. Expect the 3–5 m of open-sky GNSS error to remain in where the scene sits; the boundary band in near mode is drawn to that error for exactly this reason.

Sheet 07

Questions you'll be asked

The ones that came up in the demo, answered with numbers.

What grid size do we use?

3 × 3 m cells, fixed, for every plot. Each cell carries NDVI, NDRE and LSWI, each graded normal, moderate or critical. Touching moderate or critical cells form a patch, and each patch of two or more cells becomes one drop point — see FIG 11.

How does the camera know where it is?

On the earth, from GNSS — and held still once the agent comes within 10 m of a boundary, where the app anchors itself to the nearest point on it. From there ARCore tracks how the phone moves, to centimetres, from feature points in the image and the motion sensors. North comes from lining up that tracked walk with the GNSS fixes taken along it. The camera does not recognise fields or fences; it measures its own motion and the depth of what is in front of it. Without ARCore the camera is only a backdrop and the gyroscope and counted steps do the work.

How is distance computed?

Convert both positions into the local metric frame — multiply the latitude difference by 110,540 and the longitude difference by cos(lat) × 111,320 — then take the hypotenuse. The label on screen shows range to each plot's centroid; the near/far mode switch uses the perpendicular distance to the nearest edge, which is the number that matters when you are walking up to a boundary.

How accurate is it, honestly?

Position: 3–5 m in the open, the receiver's own accuracy, which the app displays. The automatic boundary anchor stops it wandering and ARCore holds it from there, but it cannot remove the error of the fix it was chosen from — a surveyed access point could, and that is the trade this flow makes for having no button. Heading: a few degrees once the walk has set it, 15–25° on the compass alone. Indoors GNSS is ±50–100 m and the app falls back to the compass for direction. Since sideways error is range × sin(heading error), expect roughly 1–2 m of lateral error at 30 m on a good lock. At the boundary itself the accuracy band is drawn to the receiver's live figure rather than a crisp line, so the drawing never claims more than it has.

How big is the app, and what does a visit cost in data?

The standalone release APK is 9.1 MB for arm64; embedded in Cropin Grow the plugin adds the ARCore client library and a few hundred kilobytes of its own. All seven boundaries are 1.1 kB gzipped. A crop-condition raster was 0.8–8.4 kB gzipped with one index; with three indices per 3 m cell, expect roughly twice that. A full sync of the current dataset is about 25 kB. A plot visit after sync transfers nothing at all — the AR screen never opens a socket.

Does it need internet? Does it need ARCore?

Internet only to sync, and only until the cache is warm. ARCore is used where Google Play Services for AR is installed — motion tracking, floor and depth, all on the device — and the app offers to install it where it is missing. It is not required: on handsets without it the AR screen falls back to rotation sensors and counted steps, on any Android 7.0 phone with a gyroscope and GNSS.

Is the crop-health data real?

No, and the system says so everywhere. It is deterministic synthetic NDVI, NDRE and LSWI seeded from the plot id — believable values, mostly normal with a few problem patches — present so the whole path from raster to patch to a drop point in the camera could be built and demonstrated. The API returns synthetic: true with an explanatory note and the app labels it "demo values" on screen. Connecting real Sentinel-2 means replacing one function; every consumer downstream takes an index sampler and knows nothing about its source.

What happens when the heading is bad?

The chip says so and names the source. On the sensor path the walls dim and grey. On the ARCore path the plots keep their colour and walking sets the direction by itself; where GPS is too weak for that, direction stays on the compass and the chip says so. In this build drop points stay on whatever the heading, so the flow can be tested indoors — read the chip before walking to one.

Where did the access point go?

It was removed. The app needs one known point to hold the view still, and it now uses the nearest point on any plot's boundary, chosen automatically once GPS puts the agent within 10 m of it. There is no button to press and nothing to survey when drawing a plot. The cost is honesty about accuracy: see "How accurate is it".

Why only red and yellow?

Because the view answers "where do I need to go". Normal ground is most of every plot; drawing it green across several plots would bury the walls and the problem patches under a carpet. Normal cells still appear on the web map, where the whole picture is the point.

Why does the boundary disappear behind people?

Occlusion, from ARCore's depth: anything clearly nearer than the boundary hides it, as a real fence would be hidden. It is only ghosted, never removed, so it — and the drop points — stay findable through a crop row. It works to about 8 m and on phones that support depth.

Can the phone edit a boundary?

No, by design. One writer means no merge conflicts over where a fence runs. If a boundary is wrong, it is redrawn in the web tool and picked up on the next sync.

Sheet 08

Running it yourself

Web

  1. npm install at plot-web/ — one npm workspace root covering shared, service and web.
  2. npm run build:shared first, always. The other two workspaces compile against its emitted types, not its source.
  3. npm run dev:api and npm run dev:web in two terminals. With no Supabase credentials in the environment the API falls back to SQLite, so it runs with nothing to provision.
  4. Run service/sql/003_updated_flow.sql once in the Supabase SQL editor before deploying this flow — it deletes every plot and drops the access-point column, so the data starts fresh. Then npm run seed for demo plots if wanted.
  5. npm run test:api for the API tests. node tools/gen-palette.mjs --check fails the build if either generated palette has drifted from palette.json.

Android (Flutter)

  1. Flutter 3.22 or newer. flutter pub get at the repository root (the plugin) and in example/ (the standalone app).
  2. flutter analyze — infos are fatal in CI — and flutter test for all 176 tests. No emulator, no device.
  3. cd example && flutter build apk --release --target-platform android-arm64. The API defaults to the deployed service; override it with --dart-define=PLOT_API_BASE_URL=https://your-api/.
  4. Every push to the flutter or flutter_updated_flow branch builds a debug APK and a release arm64 APK in GitHub Actions; download the plot-finder-release-arm64-apk artifact, unzip it, and install the APK from a file manager.
  5. To embed in another app, follow INTEGRATION.md: one dependency, one PlotFinderScreen push.

In the field

  1. Sync while you still have signal. Plots within 5 km, plus their crop-condition rasters.
  2. Open the app: it syncs by itself if the phone's plots are missing or over an hour old, or tap Sync now. Then tap Start AR view. The loader ticks off camera, motion tracking and GPS; move the phone gently side to side if motion tracking is waiting. Every plot within 400 m is drawn.
  3. Follow the pill to the nearest boundary. On the way, the heading chip turns green ("from your walk") without any tap.
  4. Until then you see boundaries and drop points only. Within 10 m of a boundary the sheet says Anchored to the … boundary, nothing to press, and the red and yellow ground cells appear.
  5. Pick crop greenness, crop vigour or water stress in the sheet. Red drop points are critical patches, yellow moderate; the minimap shows them all around you — tap it to enlarge.
  6. Read the chip before trusting the overlay. Indoors GPS is ±50–100 m and the plots will sit wherever that puts them; the drop points are still drawn so the flow can be tried at a desk.
