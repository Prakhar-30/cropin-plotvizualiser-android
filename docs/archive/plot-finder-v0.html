Plot Finder

A field agent stands at a roadside in front of six identical fields and has to know which one is the plot on their worksheet. Plot Finder draws that plot's boundary on the phone's camera view — offline, on ordinary hardware, and without pretending to be more accurate than it is.

01

Problem statement

Organisations that lend against farmland, insure crops, or buy produce need someone to physically visit a specific plot. The plot exists as a polygon in a database. On the ground it is one of several adjacent fields that look identical — same crop, same colour, no fences, no signs, no addresses.

Today the agent arrives at the nearest road with a set of coordinates and a blue dot on a map app. That dot tells them roughly where they are. It does not tell them where the boundary is, and at 80 m it does not resolve which of two neighbouring fields the polygon belongs to. So the agent guesses, asks a passer-by, or calls the office.

The costs of that guess are real:

  • Wrong-plot visits. An assessment recorded against the neighbour's field is worse than no assessment, because nobody knows it is wrong.
  • Time. Minutes per visit spent orienting, multiplied across a route of eight to twelve plots a day.
  • Disputes. When a farmer challenges a boundary reading, there is no record of how confident the reading was.

The obvious fix — survey-grade GNSS — costs more per unit than the phone, needs training, needs charging, and gets left in the office. Any solution that requires hardware in the field will not be used.

02

User story

Primary story

As a field agent dispatched to a farm plot,
I want to see the plot's boundary through my phone's camera when I arrive at the road,
so that I can walk to the right field with confidence, without calling the office or asking a neighbour.

Supporting stories

  • As a field agent working where there is no mobile data, I want the plots I was assigned to already be on my phone, so that losing signal does not end my day.
  • As a field agent, I want to be told when the app is not confident about direction, so that I do not trust an overlay that is pointing at the wrong field.
  • As a surveyor in the back office, I want to draw plot boundaries on satellite imagery and have the area computed for me, so that what the agent sees matches what the record says.
  • As an operations manager, I want to know how accurate this actually is across our real device fleet, so that I can decide whether to invest further.

05

Solution overview

Two pieces. A web tool where a surveyor draws plot boundaries on satellite imagery, and an Android app that shows one of those boundaries through the camera when the agent is standing near it.

5.1 How the two halves connect

5.2 Marking a plot (web tool)

The surveyor clicks corners on Esri satellite imagery, double-clicks to close, and drags vertices to adjust. Area in hectares and acres updates live as they draw. They then place a second, separate pin — the access point, where someone would walk or drive in from the road — and add a landmark note such as "transformer pole at NE corner".

On save, the server recomputes the geodesic area itself rather than trusting the browser, and rejects anything that is not a valid plot: an unclosed ring, fewer than three distinct corners, a self-intersecting shape, or anything over 500 hectares, which is almost always a mis-draw.

5.3 Finding a plot (Android)

Three screens: a list of cached plots sorted by distance, a detail screen with the boundary sketch and landmark note, and the AR view. The agent taps Find this plot, points the phone, and sees the boundary drawn on the ground with a vertical post at each corner.

5.4 The measurement, in metres

All geometry is converted once from latitude and longitude into a local east-north-up frame in metres, anchored at the agent's current position and re-anchored whenever they walk more than 50 m. Nothing downstream ever works in degrees — at this latitude a degree of longitude is 108,560 m and a degree of latitude is 110,540 m, so anything computed in raw degrees is wrong by a different amount in each direction.

Points are projected with the camera's actual field of view, read from the hardware rather than assumed. On the test device that is 60.5° across a portrait viewport; a hardcoded 60° guess would have stretched the whole outline about the centre of the image.

5.5 Heading is the whole problem

This is the part the product lives or dies on, and it is worth being precise about why. Two kinds of error can misplace the overlay, and they are not remotely equal.

A phone compass in a farmyard is wrong by 15–25°, and it is wrong silently. So the app does not trust it. It gets direction from two better sources and tells the agent which one it is using.

SourceTypical errorHow it is obtainedWhy it is trustworthy
Course over ground 3–5° Agent walks 20 m in a straight line Derived from satellite motion, so a tractor or transformer cannot corrupt it
Landmark snap 2–3° Agent aims the crosshair at the plot's access point and taps Both coordinates are known, so the true bearing is computed outright
Magnetometer 15–25° The phone compass Fallback only — never a resting state

Once direction is fixed by a walk or a snap, it is carried by the gyroscope. The compass is allowed only to correct slow drift, over a five-minute time constant and with a hard ceiling of 10° on how far it may ever pull a good lock. Without that ceiling, a magnetically disturbed site would drag the heading onto the neighbouring field slowly enough that nobody would notice.

5.8 What was deliberately not built

v0 does not use ARCore's Geospatial API, which is the obvious thing to reach for and the wrong one. It depends on Google's Visual Positioning System, which needs Street View coverage that farmland does not have. It compiles, it reports sub-2 metre accuracy, and in a field it is wrong by 5–10 m with a 30–45° heading error — while still reporting confidence. Silent wrongness is the one failure this product cannot afford.

Pose is instead read from the phone's own rotation sensors, behind an interface that an ARCore implementation could satisfy later without changing anything above it. Also out of scope for v0: boundary correction by the agent, photo capture, audit forms, and iOS.

5.9 Measuring itself

Every AR session writes one row to a local table — position, reported accuracy, heading, which source produced it, render mode, session length — and uploads it when there is signal. No UI, by design. This is what turns "it seemed to work" into a real accuracy distribution across the actual device fleet, and it is the evidence any decision about phase 2 should rest on.

Where it does not apply

  • Legal boundary determination. This is an identification aid at metre scale, not a cadastral survey instrument.
  • Dense forest or steep terrain. Geometry assumes flat ground at the device's altitude; there is no terrain model.
  • Very small plots. Below roughly 10 m across, GNSS uncertainty is comparable to the plot itself.
  • Indoor or covered sites. Needs a usable satellite fix.

The one number that matters most

Heading lock rate. If agents routinely end up on the compass, the overlay is greyed out and the product is not delivering — and the fix is a product and training question, not an accuracy question.

Honest status

v0 has been validated once, by one person, on one high-end phone, in one location. That is enough to prove the approach works and not enough to predict how it behaves across a fleet of mid-range devices in monsoon conditions. The next milestone is a multi-device pilot, and the telemetry to judge it by is already built.
