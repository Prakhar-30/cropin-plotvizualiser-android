The problem

Field agents struggle to locate plots and their boundaries, and to identify exactly which part of a plot needs attention in terms of crop health. Standing at the edge of an unmarked field, there is nothing to tell you where one holding ends and the next begins, and nothing to tell you which corner of it is failing.

An AR boundary and an in-place health overlay make both answerable at a glance: the agent looks through the phone and sees the limit drawn on the ground in front of them, with the stressed ground shaded and the worst patch flagged to walk to.

The shape of the system

Three pieces, and a deliberate one-way street between them: boundaries are drawn on the web and nowhere else, so the phone is never a source of truth it cannot verify.

Why this way

Plots are created on the web and never on the phone. A boundary drawn by someone standing in the middle of a field on a ±5 m fix would be a boundary accurate to ±5 m; one traced against satellite imagery is accurate to the imagery. The phone's job is to find a boundary, which is a much easier problem than defining one.

What was deliberately left out

Not usedWhy not
ARCore Geospatial Needs Play Services, a network round trip to Google's VPS, and street-level imagery coverage. Farmland has none of the three. It would also make the app unusable exactly where it is needed.
SLAM / visual-inertial tracking Needs texture to track. A ploughed field at midday is a repeating pattern under a blown-out sky — the worst case for feature matching, and it fails silently by drifting rather than by stopping.
A 3D engine Every point is reduced to screen coordinates before drawing. Pulling in a scene graph to fill a translucent quadrilateral is several hundred times the dependency for identical pixels.
RTK / external GNSS Would fix the accuracy problem outright, and requires hardware the agent does not carry. The design target is the phone already in their pocket.

The libraries

Short list on purpose. Everything geometric is hand-written, because the geometry is the product and a dependency that gets it subtly wrong is a dependency you cannot debug.

On the phone

Camera

CameraX

Google's camera API. Gives a preview surface and, critically, the real CameraCharacteristics — sensor size and focal length, from which the true field of view is computed (60.5° on this handset).

Guessing the FOV instead would scale every projected point by a constant error, which looks like the plot being the wrong size.

Interface

Jetpack Compose

Declarative UI toolkit. The overlay is a single Canvas composited over the preview; state flows in, strokes and fills come out.

Chosen over Views because the overlay is redrawn on every pose sample and Compose makes that a pure function of state.

Cache

Room

SQLite with compile-time-checked queries and real migrations. Holds plots and plot_health.

Migrations matter more than they look: the cache is the only copy of a boundary an agent has once offline, so an upgrade must never wipe it.

Network

Retrofit + OkHttp

Typed HTTP client. OkHttp transparently requests and decompresses gzip, which is why a 71 KB raster costs 8 KB on the wire.

Used only inside Sync. Nothing on a screen ever waits on it.

Serialisation

kotlinx.serialization

Compile-time JSON. ignoreUnknownKeys means the server can add fields without breaking installed apps.

Reflection-based parsers cost startup time and fail at runtime rather than at build time.

Sensors

Android SensorManager

Two rotation vectors, and the fused location provider. No third-party wrapper — the fusion logic here is the interesting part and belongs in code that can be unit-tested on a plain JVM.

All 125 tests run without a device or an emulator.

On the web and the server

Map

MapLibre GL JS

Open-source vector map renderer, with Esri World Imagery as the raster basemap. The surveyor traces the boundary against what the field actually looks like from above.

Geometry

Turf.js

Geospatial predicates and operations. Used for booleanPointInPolygon, intersect and area when rasterising the health grid.

Trusted here and not on the phone because the raster is built once, server-side, where a millisecond does not matter.

Database

PostGIS on Supabase

Boundaries are stored as geography(Polygon,4326), so ST_Area and ST_DWithin are computed on the spheroid rather than approximated.

The “plots near me” query is an index-backed ST_DWithin, not a bounding-box scan.

Shared

@plot/shared

Our own TypeScript workspace holding validation, severity banding and the health rasteriser — used by the map, the API, and any future ingest.

A cell graded amber on the map and stressed in the database would be worse than no colour at all.

Why this way

One palette file, palette.json, generates both the TypeScript and the Kotlin colour tables. Hand-copying eight hex strings across a language boundary is exactly the kind of thing that drifts, and a plot drawn amber in the web tool and cyan in the AR view is worse than having no colours at all.

The logic, stage by stage

This is a genuine sequence: each stage consumes the previous one's output, and runs in full for every frame the camera delivers.

01

Degrees become metres

A boundary arrives as WGS84 latitude and longitude. Nothing can be drawn in those units — a degree of latitude is 110,540 m everywhere, but a degree of longitude is 111,320 m × cos(latitude), which at Bengaluru's 12.9°N is 108,500 m. Treating them as interchangeable would stretch every plot by two percent along one axis.

So an East-North-Up frame is anchored at the agent's position, and every point is converted into metres relative to it. The frame re-anchors when the agent walks far enough that the flat-earth approximation would start to cost more than a few centimetres.

02

Where the camera is

A phone GNSS fix wanders by several metres even when the phone is lying still on a wall. Drawn straight, the boundary jitters by that amount every second, which reads as the field sliding around.

Three filters run in series. A zero-velocity detector decides whether the agent is walking, using Doppler speed when the receiver reports it. A constant-position Kalman filter then folds each fix in, opening its process noise when walking and clamping it when still, and rejecting fixes more than three sigma from the estimate. Finally — and this is the piece added after the second field test — a slew limiter separates the number the filter believes from the position actually drawn.

Why this way

An unbiased estimator is the right goal for an estimator and the wrong one for something a person is looking at. The slew limiter trades absolute accuracy for stillness on purpose: a boundary sitting half a metre out but perfectly steady reads as correct, while one that is unbiased and never settles reads as broken. It snaps rather than crawls if the gap exceeds 10 m, because suppressing noise must never become hiding the truth.

03

Which way it points

Heading is the hardest input, and the one that matters most: a position error moves the overlay by a fixed number of metres, but an orientation error moves it by an amount that grows with range. One degree is 0.87 m at 50 m and 2.6 m at 150 m.

A phone compass is wrong by 15–25° near steel, a running motor, or a metal gate — all of which are common exactly where plots are. So the compass is never trusted to set the heading. Two things are: a walked segment, whose bearing is geometry rather than magnetism, and a landmark snap, where the agent aims the crosshair at the plot's known access point and the true bearing is computed outright.

04

Taking the shimmer out

Android's rotation vector wobbles by a degree or two even on a phone lying on a table. Left raw, that is several metres of visible movement at range — more than the GNSS jitter fixed in stage 02.

The smoother interpolates on quaternions, not matrices: averaging rotation matrices element-wise does not produce a rotation, and shears the overlay instead of turning it. Its time constant is adaptive, following the 1-euro filter (Casiez, Roussel & Vogel, CHI 2012) — heavy when the phone is nearly still, light when it is being deliberately turned.

The trick is what it adapts on. Adapting on frame-to-frame angular rate fails: jitter of 1.5° arriving every 20 ms is an instantaneous 75°/s, indistinguishable from a pan. Adapting on deviation from the current estimate separates them cleanly, because jitter is bounded by its own amplitude no matter how fast it arrives, while a genuine turn makes the estimate fall behind and the gap grows until the filter opens up.

05

Onto the screen

With a camera position and an orientation, projection is a pinhole model: rotate each ENU point into the device frame, divide by depth, scale by the focal length in pixels. Focal length comes from the camera's real reported field of view, so the overlay matches the preview exactly.

The part that cannot be skipped is near-plane clipping. A point behind the camera has negative depth, and dividing by it projects the point to a mirrored position in front — a boundary corner appearing in precisely the wrong direction. So every polygon is clipped against a plane just in front of the lens (Sutherland–Hodgman), which introduces new vertices where edges cross it.

The bug this replaced

For a while the overlay “moved along with the user”. The cause was not noise: the projection was being handed raw ENU coordinates with the camera left at the frame origin, so the entire scene translated whenever the agent walked. Making the camera a required parameter of the projection function made the class of mistake impossible to repeat.

06

Deciding what to draw

The right drawing changes with range, so there are two modes and a set of distance fades between them. Beyond 30 m the shape is what matters — a pin tells you nothing about which of six identical fields you are looking at, while an outline you can match against a hedge line confirms it. Inside 30 m a crisp line would be a false precision claim, so each edge becomes a band as wide as the live GNSS accuracy.

Boundaries are drawn as standing walls rather than flat outlines. A shape lying on the ground reads as a carpet, not a limit — and it disappears entirely when the phone is held level, because the ground plane is then edge-on to the camera. Walls run from the earth to one metre above eye height, so the top rail sits against the sky and the panel unmistakably stands on the ground.

07

The crop-health raster

Health is graded server-side and shipped as cells, so the map and the phone can never disagree about which ground is stressed. Clients receive a band, not a raw index to threshold themselves — otherwise every client owns a copy of the agronomy and they drift apart.

Why this way

Opacity rises with severity because healthy ground is context — it must not paint over the crop the agent came to look at — while a critical patch is the answer to the question and is allowed to be assertive. Cell size scales to the plot, targeting about fifteen cells across, floored at 2 m and never coarser than Sentinel-2's native 10 m.

What the field found

The ramp originally ended in amber and brown, chosen so the bad end would describe the ground rather than alarm about it. Outdoors that reasoning failed on its own terms: amber and brown are the colour of dry soil, so the two worst bands painted earth onto earth and vanished. A grading layer has to contrast with the ground it grades.

What field testing found

Every one of these passed its unit tests. They are recorded because the pattern is the useful part: each was invisible in the lab and obvious in a field.

Two sensors, one filter

The pose source ran both of Android's rotation vectors through a single smoother. The two do not agree — the game rotation vector's yaw origin is arbitrary at boot — so the filter saw a stream alternating between two attitudes tens of degrees apart at roughly 100 Hz.

What that breaks is worth stating precisely, because the obvious guess is wrong. It does not corrupt the drawn pitch and roll directly. It saturates the deviation measure — the very signal that distinguishes a deliberate turn from jitter. Pinned at 33°, it reports “always turning”, holds the time constant at its most agile 0.05 s, and the smoother silently stops smoothing. Every number downstream still looked entirely plausible.

Compounding multipliers

Three independent fades multiplied into the final alpha, each reasonable alone. Together they made the layer invisible.

What this means at scale

A full district sync of 100 plots would be roughly 400 KB — under a second on any connection, and about the size of one photograph. The binding constraint is not bandwidth; it is that Sync has to happen before the agent loses signal. Everything after that point is free.

Using the system

Two halves: someone marks a plot at a desk, someone else finds it in a field. They never need to be the same person.

Marking a plot — on the web

  1. Open the tool and press “Use my location” before drawing anything. A plot drawn on the map's default view lands wherever that view happens to be — which is how a boundary once ended up 97 km from the person who drew it, invisible to a phone that only caches what is nearby.
  2. Trace the boundary against the satellite imagery, corner by corner. Close the ring. The server recomputes area and centroid on the spheroid, so no client can report a wrong figure.
  3. Place the access point and write a landmark note for it — “gate by the culvert”, “opposite side of the open road”. This is the single most useful field the form has: it is what the agent aims at to pin their heading.
  4. Save. A palette slot is assigned so that no two plots within 500 m share a colour.
Watch for

A stray corner produces a thin spike across the plot — harmless on a map, but in AR it becomes a wall standing through the middle of the field. If the shape on the detail screen has a spur running to a point inside itself, redraw it.

Finding it — in the field

  1. Press Sync while you still have signal. This is the only moment the app needs a network. It caches every plot near you together with its health raster — about 24 KB.
  2. Pick the plot from the list, which is ordered by distance, and press Find this plot.
  3. Pin the heading. Either walk twenty metres in a straight line, which locks it automatically from your track, or aim the crosshair at the access point and tap Aim at it and tap to pin heading. The snap refuses if you are standing almost on top of the access point, because a bearing between two points a couple of metres apart is noise.
  4. Read the chip, top left. It is the honest statement of how much to trust what you are seeing.
  5. Cycle the health layer with the button, bottom left: Problem areas → All cells → Health off. It starts on problem areas, which hides healthy ground so the stressed patches stand alone.
  6. Walk to the numbered stems. Marker 1 is the worst patch. They stand 2.5 m tall specifically so they clear waist-high crop, which would hide anything painted at ankle height.

If something looks wrong

  • No colour on the ground. Check the chip — at Unreliable the health layer is deliberately suppressed and says so. Otherwise the raster may not be cached; press Sync.
  • The boundary sits beside the real fence. Read the ± figure on the label. That is the live GNSS accuracy, and the boundary cannot be better than it. Re-snap the heading; if it persists, the offset is position, not heading.
  • Nothing appears at all. An arrow at the screen edge points toward the plot when it is out of frame. If there is no arrow, you are likely more than 5 km from anything cached.
  • “Synthetic NDVI” in orange. The health values are a deterministic stand-in, not satellite measurements. Everything about the pipeline is real except the numbers; swapping in Sentinel-2 means replacing one sampler function.
